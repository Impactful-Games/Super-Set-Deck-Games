== Gameplay

B.O.N is a trick-taking game, similar to the Hearts you used to play on your computer at work.
Or the Pinochle your grandma taught you.
Grandma used to put warmed-up syrup on your pancakes too; that was pretty great.

Anyway, B.O.N is played over a number of rounds.
Points are assigned to the players after each round.
The first player to score at least as many points as there are players is the undisputed Champion of Boasting, and can lord it over all the other players for all time.

Determine a start player by any method you choose.
We suggest the player who most recently had an adult beverage.
If you still can’t figure out a start player, pick whoever offers to wash your car.
Or have a steel cage total brawl.
Or whatever.
Just start the game already.
Jeez.

The lucky soul chosen as the start player plays any one card from their hand.
The color of this card determines the Prime Color.

Play always moves clockwise (because who makes a card game that plays counterclockwise? c’mon now), and the following players must play a card of the Prime Color if they can.
If a player cannot play the appropriate color, they may play any card from their hand.

If the start player leads with a Butterfly, the first card of a color played sets the Prime Color.
Also, why you gotta be so difficult, Brad?

After each player has played one card (this is called a Trick!), it’s time to determine the card with the highest value.
Once you have determined the highest-valued card, the player who played it takes the Trick and leads the next Trick.


== Determining the highest value

This is the best part of the game.
Seriously.
Pay attention.

OK.
If all the played cards match the Prime Color, then the highest numerical-valued card wins.
Easy enough.

But if a card is played that doesn’t match the Prime Color, then it’s time to consult the Color Rank cards!
The cards are ranked from highest to lowest, top to bottom.
Top, good.
Bottom, bad.
Middle... um, middle.
The highest numerically valued card of the highest-ranked color played always wins the Trick.
Make sense?
How about an example?

.Trick
====
The Color Rank cards are arranged top to bottom: ♠, ♥, ♣.

Bill leads with ♥8.
Sue follows with ♥3.
Julie plays ♠1.
Greg plays ♣9.

♥ is the Prime Color, with 8 as the highest card.
But we have some off-color cards, so let’s check the Color Ranks.
♠ is on top, so any ♠ card of any numerical value beats any ♥ or ♣ card.

Greg has the highest numerical value in the Trick, but it’s ♣.
Better luck next time, Greg.
Julie played ♠1, and even though it’s the lowest numerical value, ♠ is currently stronger than the other colors, so Julie takes the Trick.
Scoop it up, Julie.
You’ll lead the next Trick.
====

So, the hierarchy is:

. the color of the cards, and then
. the numerical value of the cards within the strongest color played.

If it’s still unclear, please look us up on IG, TW, or FB and tell us how awful we are at conveying simple mechanics via the written word.

NOW!
Here’s the really tricky part of this “Tricky Trick-taking Game”...

You ready for this?
Any time a color wins a Trick, that color’s Color Rank card is moved to the bottom of the stack.
Cool, huh?

.Color rank change
====
So in our scenario above, the ♠ card would move to the bottom, and the new ranking would be ♥, ♣, ♠.
Is your mind blown yet?
We thought so.
====


== Butterflies

“That’s all great,” you say, “but what about these Butterfly cards we have?
What’s the deal there?”

Glad you asked.
We were getting to those next.
Butterfly cards are pretty simple, really.
A player can play a Butterfly at any time, whether or not they have cards of the Prime Color in their hand.
And Butterflies are always the weakest cards of any Trick.

== End of the round

At the end of each round, players are assigned points based on the Tricks they’ve taken.
But because the judges of the World Championships of Boasting are a little sauced, the scoring is kinda wonky.

Here’s how many Tricks each player needs to take, according to the number of players (in case you missed it in the setup chart):

* 3 players: 3 Tricks.
* 4 players: 2 Tricks.
* 5 players: 1 Trick.

Make sense?
Great.
So if you’re in a 3-player game, each player who takes EXACTLY 3 Tricks scores 1 point.
2 Tricks for a 4-player game.
And 1 Trick for a 5-player game.
That’s not so hard!

BUT!
If you take zero Tricks, regardless of the number of players, you score 2 points.
Because you’re too cool for school: you know it, the audience knows it, and the judges definitely know it.
Take your two points, Fonz.

.Points
====
In a 4-player game, if you took:

* 0 Tricks: you score 2 points.
* 1 Trick: you don’t score any points.
* 2 Tricks: you score 1 point.
* 3 or more Tricks: you don’t score any points.
====

The game ends when a player scores at least as many points as there are players.
If several players reach that threshold at the same time, the player who owns this game wins.
No.
Sorry.
Not that.
If several players reach the threshold at the same time, the player with the most points wins.

In case of a tie, the player who took the most cards of the strongest color (the top Color Rank card) in the last round is the winner.
If it is still a tie, the player who took the most cards of the second strongest color in the last round is the winner.
If it is still a tie, then clearly you two are doppelgangers and one of you is in real trouble.

Now celebrate that win with a shot of your favorite hard stuff.
And put it on their tab.
